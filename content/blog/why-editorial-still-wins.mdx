The dominant aesthetic of the web in 2026 is the dashboard. Tiles. Pills. Cards. A thousand panels of equal hierarchy, each politely demanding attention. It is the visual language of *productivity software*, and it has quietly colonised everything — even sites that have nothing to do with productivity.

When I open a portfolio that feels arranged like a SaaS app, my eyes glaze over before I scroll. Not because the work is bad, but because the *frame* is too familiar.

## What editorial does differently

Editorial design — the language of magazines, books, and broadsheets — is built on a different assumption: that the reader is here on purpose. That they will give the page time. That hierarchy can be expressed not by size alone, but by spacing, type weight, italic, and silence.

A magazine spread is unafraid of empty white space because it trusts the reader to find their way. A dashboard cannot afford that trust — every pixel must justify itself.

## Three things I steal from print

1. **The pull quote**. One sentence, larger than the body, drawn out of the prose. It tells you what to remember.
2. **The numbered section**. Roman numerals, small caps, set apart. It implies a curated sequence.
3. **The marginalia**. Tiny labels in the gutter — dates, locations, captions. They make the page feel *edited*, not assembled.

> A page that feels edited is a page that feels considered. Considered is the most underrated quality in design.

## The trade-off

Editorial layouts are harder to maintain. They reward attention to typography that most templates can't accommodate. They cannot be CMS'd into oblivion.

But the work that wins — the work that lives in screenshots, in moodboards, in the back of someone's mind a year later — almost always borrows from print.

If you want your site to feel like *yours*, stop thinking of it as a UI. Start thinking of it as a publication.
