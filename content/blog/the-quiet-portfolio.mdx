I look at a lot of portfolios. It is, embarrassingly, my favourite genre of internet. And the ones I remember are almost never the loudest ones.

The portfolios that stick are *quiet*. They have one good idea, executed completely. They don't try to demonstrate everything the author can do. They demonstrate one specific feeling.

## The temptation

When you sit down to build your own portfolio, the temptation is to put everything in. Every project. Every skill. Every animation library you've ever used. Each section a small flex.

The result is a website that reads like a CV with feelings.

## The cure

Pick the three projects that you are most proud of. Cut the rest. Pick the one thing about your work that you are best at. Make the entire site about that.

> A portfolio is not a record of what you have done. It is an argument for what you should do next.

If you are a motion designer, the portfolio should feel like a moving image. If you are a typographer, the portfolio should be a type specimen. If you are a generalist, the portfolio should be the case for hiring a generalist — not a buffet of evidence.

## What I left out

This site, if you are reading it on launch day, has no awards page. No client logos. No "trusted by." Those things are fine, in context. They are not what makes someone want to work with you.

What makes someone want to work with you is the feeling that you have a point of view. That you would not have made the same site they would have. That the way you arranged this one will tell them how you would arrange theirs.

That is the only argument that matters.
